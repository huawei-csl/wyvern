1. Introduction

1.1 Nano-Drone Characteristics and Applications

Nano-drones, characterized by their compact size (under 10 cm) and lightweight (less than 40 g), have emerged as a transformative technology for applications in GPS-denied environments, confined spaces, and proximity to humans [1, IEEE - Nano-UAVs Introduction]. Their small form factor enables them to navigate cluttered indoor environments, such as industrial plants or collapsed buildings, making them ideal for tasks like search-and-rescue, inspection, and surveillance [2, IEEE - Nano-Drones CNNs]. Additionally, their ability to operate safely near humans and access narrow spaces has opened up new possibilities in fields such as precision agriculture, aerial photography, and infrastructure monitoring [3, IEEE - Nano-UAVs and AI Integration]. Despite their potential, nano-drones face significant challenges due to their simplified sensory, mechanical, and computational subsystems, which limit their ability to perform resource-intensive tasks autonomously [1, IEEE - Nano-UAVs Introduction]. For instance, their small size restricts the payload capacity, often limiting the sensors and computational hardware that can be onboarded [4, IEEE - Nano-UAVs].

1.2 Computational, Memory, and Power Constraints

The miniaturization of nano-drones imposes stringent constraints on their computational power, memory capacity, and energy consumption. Typically, nano-drones operate with computational resources of less than 100 milliwatts, significantly lower than those of standard-sized commercial drones equipped with powerful CPUs or GPUs [1, IEEE - Nano-UAVs Introduction]. This limitation necessitates the use of Microcontroller Unit (MCU)-class processors, which struggle to execute complex perception, planning, and control algorithms [4, IEEE - Nano-UAVs]. Furthermore, the small physical footprint restricts the size of the battery and printed circuit board, leaving only a fraction of the power envelope available for computation [4, IEEE - Nano-UAVs]. These constraints make it challenging to deploy real-time navigation pipelines directly onboard, often requiring offloading tasks to external servers, which introduces issues such as unstable wireless connections, limited range, and unpredictable response latency [1, IEEE - Nano-UAVs Introduction]. For example, the Crazyflie 2.1 nano-drone, equipped with a GAP8 System-on-Chip (SoC), exemplifies these challenges, as it must balance computational tasks with a power budget of just a few Watts [5, Palossi-PULP-DroNet].

1.3 Role of Deep Neural Networks in Nano-Drone Autonomy

Deep Neural Networks (DNNs) have emerged as a critical enabler of autonomy in nano-drones, offering efficient solutions for perception, navigation, and control tasks. Unlike traditional model-based approaches, such as Model Predictive Control (MPC), which are computationally intensive and impractical for nano-drones, DNNs provide a more efficient alternative in terms of latency and power consumption [6, IEEE - Optimization of Deep Neural Networks for Nano-Drones]. For instance, convolutional neural networks (CNNs) have been widely adopted for perception tasks on nano-drones, replacing more complex geometrical computer vision pipelines [2, IEEE - Nano-Drones CNNs]. However, deploying DNNs on nano-drones requires significant optimization to meet their computational, memory, and power constraints. Techniques such as Neural Architecture Search (NAS), quantization, and early-exit mechanisms have been employed to reduce model size, latency, and energy consumption while maintaining accuracy [7, RissoNAS] [8, BLANKSKIP] [9, AdaptiveDL-NanoDrones]. For example, NAS algorithms like Supernet and PIT have been used to optimize DNN architectures for visual pose estimation, achieving significant reductions in latency and memory usage [7, RissoNAS]. Additionally, adaptive deep learning mechanisms, such as those proposed in [9, AdaptiveDL-NanoDrones], allow nano-drones to dynamically switch between DNN models based on operational conditions, further enhancing efficiency. These advancements have enabled nano-drones to perform complex tasks autonomously, paving the way for their integration into diverse applications, from agile flight control to 3D scene reconstruction [5, Palossi-PULP-DroNet] [10, Tiny-DroNeRF].

3.1 Neural Architecture Search Methods

3.1.1 Supernet Algorithm

The Supernet algorithm is a Neural Architecture Search (NAS) method that enables the exploration of optimal DNN architectures by selecting between different layer types based on trainable parameters. This approach is particularly useful for nano-drones, where computational, memory, and power constraints necessitate highly efficient models. The Supernet algorithm evaluates various layer configurations, such as DepthWise (DW) and PointWise (PW) convolutions, standard 2D convolutions, or even no-operation layers, as part of a broader optimization pipeline. The selection is guided by a loss function that balances task-specific performance (e.g., Mean Squared Error) with model complexity (e.g., number of parameters or operations) [7, RissoNAS]. 3.1.2 PIT Algorithm for Structured Pruning

The PIT (Pruning in Training) algorithm complements the Supernet approach by performing fine-grained structured pruning. It trains binary masks to eliminate unimportant output channels, thereby reducing the model’s computational and memory footprint. This method is particularly effective for nano-drones, where even small reductions in model complexity can lead to substantial improvements in latency. The combination of Supernet and PIT algorithms provides a powerful framework for optimizing DNN architectures for resource-constrained platforms like nano-drones.

3.2 Quantization and Precision Reduction

Quantization and precision reduction are critical techniques for optimizing DNNs on nano-drones, where memory and computational resources are severely limited. By reducing the numerical representation of weights and activations from 32-bit floating point to lower precision formats (e.g., 16-bit fixed point), these techniques significantly reduce memory usage and computational complexity without sacrificing accuracy. For example, in the deployment of DroNet for autonomous navigation on nano-drones, quantization enabled the execution of complex DNNs on milliwatt-scale platforms, achieving real-time processing at 6 fps with an average power consumption of 64 mW [5, Palossi-PULP-DroNet]. Quantization is particularly effective when combined with hardware-aware optimizations, such as those implemented on the GAP8 SoC, which supports low-precision arithmetic through ISA extensions and functional units integrated into its core architecture [11, RISC-V-DNN-Optimization]. The GAP8 SoC leverages advanced RISC-V microcontrollers and octa-core accelerators optimized for digital signal processing and deep inference, enabling efficient execution of quantized models [5, Palossi-PULP-DroNet].

3.3 Slimmable and Dynamic Width Networks

Slimmable and dynamic width networks offer a flexible approach to DNN optimization by allowing the model to adjust its computational complexity at runtime. This is achieved by dynamically selecting the width of the network’s layers based on external conditions, such as remaining battery life or task difficulty. For instance, a slimmable MobileNetV2 SSDLite model was modified to support four width configurations (0.25×, 0.5×, 0.75×, 1.0×), enabling the model to adapt its resource usage to the operational context of nano-drones [14, Slimmable-Early-Exit-Nano-Drones-Marra]. This approach minimizes memory overhead while maintaining the ability to perform complex tasks like object detection, making it highly suitable for resource-constrained platforms. The shared weight mechanism ensures that the memory footprint remains minimal, making it feasible for deployment on resource-limited platforms [14, Slimmable-Early-Exit-Nano-Drones-Marra].

The Adaptive Deep Learning framework incorporates temporal consistency adaptation by comparing predictions across consecutive frames to trigger model switching. If the predicted consistency is high, the system can skip certain computations or switch to a lighter model, thereby reducing latency and energy consumption [9, AdaptiveDL-NanoDrones].

Similarly, the BLANKSKIP framework uses a content-based early-exit mechanism to identify frames that do not contain objects of interest, bypassing the full detection pipeline for such frames. This approach is particularly beneficial in scenarios where the drone is moving through an environment with sparse objects, as it allows the system to focus computational resources on frames that are more likely to contain relevant information [8, BLANKSKIP]. The content-based skipping mechanism thus plays a crucial role in optimizing the efficiency of DNN inference on nano-drones, especially in dynamic and resource-constrained environments.

5.3 Dynamic Width Configuration

Dynamic width configuration involves adjusting the width of a neural network (i.e., the number of active channels or neurons) at runtime based on the operational context. This approach allows the system to dynamically scale the computational complexity of the model, optimizing resource usage without significantly increasing memory overhead. The Slimmable and Early Exit Neural Networks framework modifies the MobileNetV2 SSDLite model into a slimmable model, enabling dynamic selection of four width configurations (0.25×, 0.5×, 0.75×, 1.0×) for layers after the 6th feature extractor block. This modification allows the model to adjust its computational complexity and resource usage based on external conditions, such as remaining battery life or expected task difficulty [14, Slimmable-Early-Exit-Nano-Drones-Marra].

The shared weight mechanism in this framework ensures that the memory footprint remains minimal, making it feasible for deployment on resource-limited platforms. However, the effectiveness of dynamic width configuration depends on the ability to accurately predict the operational context and select the appropriate width configuration. Rapid changes in external conditions may require more sophisticated width selection strategies to maintain optimal performance [14, Slimmable-Early-Exit-Nano-Drones-Marra]. Despite these challenges, dynamic width configuration offers a promising approach to optimizing DNN inference on nano-drones, enabling them to adapt to varying operational demands while conserving resources.

6. Edge Computing Integration

Edge computing integration is a critical aspect of deploying deep neural networks (DNNs) on nano-drones, as it enables real-time processing and decision-making without relying on centralized cloud infrastructure. This section explores the architectures, resource allocation strategies, and federated learning frameworks that facilitate efficient edge computing integration for nano-drones.

6.1 Dynamic Task Offloading Architectures

Dynamic task offloading architectures are essential for optimizing the computational workload on UAVs, which are constrained by limited processing power, memory, and battery life. The Dynamic Task Offloading Edge-Aware Optimization Framework (DTOE-AOF) is a notable example that dynamically allocates tasks between UAVs and edge nodes based on proximity, available resources, and task urgency [17, DTOE-AOF]. This framework leverages AI-driven decision-making, including federated learning for data privacy and reinforcement learning for real-time adaptability, to reduce latency and improve mission efficiency. By offloading computationally intensive tasks to edge nodes, DTOE-AOF conserves onboard resources and ensures that UAVs can operate efficiently in dynamic environments [18, DTOE-AOF].

The framework’s edge orchestration and load-balancing strategies distribute workloads across edge nodes, minimizing latency and bandwidth consumption. Additionally, edge caching and real-time analytics further enhance performance by reducing the need for repeated data transfers. Simulation studies demonstrate that DTOE-AOF achieves significant improvements in mission efficiency (99.4%) and response time (98.9% reduction) compared to traditional centralized methods [17, DTOE-AOF]. However, challenges such as ensuring data security during transmission and managing UAV mobility remain, highlighting the need for further optimization in dynamic task offloading architectures.

6.2 Edge-Aware Resource Allocation

The integration of AI algorithms with edge computing platforms enables real-time decision-making and dynamic resource allocation based on computational load, energy consumption, and network latency [12, FORECR - Edge AI in UAVs]. For instance, the MILBOX AGX platform, powered by NVIDIA Jetson AGX Orin, supports AI integration in UAVs by providing high-performance computing capabilities and extensive connectivity options. This platform facilitates tasks such as real-time image processing, object detection, and recognition directly from live video streams, enhancing intelligence, surveillance, and reconnaissance (ISR) operations [12, FORECR - Edge AI in UAVs].

The GAP8 System-on-Chip (SoC), for example, leverages advanced RISC-V microcontroller units and octa-core accelerators to optimize memory-side operations and reduce energy consumption [5, Palossi-PULP-DroNet]. Techniques such as tiling and parallelization are employed to optimize memory utilization and computational efficiency for edge deployment on resource-constrained platforms like nano-drones. Additionally, the use of fused kernels for executing sequences of DNN layers, such as DepthWise (DW) and PointWise (PW) convolutions, further reduces memory overhead and improves latency, as demonstrated in the optimization pipeline proposed by Risso et al. [7, RissoNAS].

6.3 Federated Learning Implementation

Federated learning (FL) is a promising approach for enabling collaborative learning among nano-drones while preserving data privacy. In FL, multiple devices train local models using their onboard data and share only the model updates with a central server or edge node, rather than transmitting raw data. This approach can be beneficial for devices operating in environments with limited connectivity [17, DTOE-AOF].

The Tiny-DroNeRF framework exemplifies the application of federated learning in nano-drones, where multiple drones collaboratively train a Neural Radiance Fields (NeRF) model to reconstruct 3D scenes. By aggregating individual NeRF instances, the framework produces richer reconstructions while minimizing communication overhead [10, Tiny-DroNeRF]. Similarly, the DTOE-AOF framework integrates federated learning to enhance data privacy during task offloading [18, DTOE-AOF].

On-device self-supervised learning approaches, such as those proposed by Cereda et al., enable nano-drones to fine-tune pre-trained models using ultra-low-power resources, reducing horizontal position error by up to 26% compared to non-fine-tuned baselines [15, Cereda-OnDeviceLearning]. These methods address the domain shift problem, where model performance degrades when deployed in environments different from the training domain.

In conclusion, edge computing integration is essential for enabling efficient and autonomous operation of nano-drones. Dynamic task offloading architectures, edge-aware resource allocation, and federated learning frameworks collectively address the computational, memory, and power constraints of nano-drones, paving the way for advanced applications in areas such as autonomous navigation, 3D scene reconstruction, and real-time object detection.

7.1 Visual Pose Estimation

Visual pose estimation is a critical task for nano-drones, enabling them to determine their position and orientation in space using onboard cameras. The optimization of deep neural networks (DNNs) for visual pose estimation on nano-drones has been a focal point of recent research. For instance, [7, RissoNAS] introduces an optimization pipeline that leverages Neural Architecture Search (NAS) algorithms, including Supernet and PIT, to balance accuracy, latency, and memory usage. The Supernet selects between different layer types (e.g., DepthWise + PointWise blocks, single PointWise layers, or standard 2D convolutions) based on trainable parameters, while PIT eliminates unimportant output channels through binary masks. The optimized models achieve significant improvements, with the smallest model achieving a 9× size compression at iso-MAE compared to the most accurate state-of-the-art (SoTA) model, while the largest model improves MAE by up to 13.78%. Additionally, [16, Jitaru - Tiny Vision Transformers] explores the use of Vision Transformers (ViTs) for human pose estimation on nano-drones, demonstrating that optimized ViTs outperform MobileNet in accuracy and generalization, achieving a 12% lower Mean Absolute Error (MAE) across all predicted axes. Structured pruning reduces the model size by 30% with only a 12% decrease in performance, making ViTs a viable alternative for real-time applications on resource-constrained platforms. These advancements highlight the potential of DNNs in enabling precise and efficient visual pose estimation on nano-drones.

8. Performance Evaluation Metrics

Latency refers to the time taken for a DNN to process an input and produce an output, while throughput measures the number of tasks the DNN can handle per unit of time. For nano-drones, low latency is essential to ensure timely decision-making, especially in dynamic environments where delays can lead to collisions or mission failure.

Several studies have demonstrated significant improvements in latency through optimization techniques. 9.1 Latency Reduction

Optimized deployment of deep neural networks (DNNs) on nano-drones significantly reduces inference latency, which is critical for real-time applications such as autonomous navigation and agile flight control. Techniques like fused layer execution, where sequences of DNN layers (e.g., convolution + pooling) are combined into single operations, minimize memory overhead and intermediate data transfers, leading to faster processing. For instance, the fused kernel developed by Risso et al. reduces inference latency by up to 3.22× compared to state-of-the-art methods while maintaining the same error rate (iso-error) [7, RissoNAS] [13, Risso-Daghero Optimized Deployment]. Similarly, adaptive deep learning mechanisms, which proactively swap between different DNN models based on temporal consistency, achieve a 28% reduction in latency while maintaining accuracy [9, AdaptiveDL-NanoDrones]. These optimizations are crucial for nano-drones, where even minor delays can impact mission-critical tasks.

9.2 Memory Footprint Compression

Memory constraints are a significant challenge for nano-drones, which typically operate with less than 10 MiB of memory. Optimized deployment strategies, such as structured pruning and quantization, drastically reduce the memory footprint of DNNs without compromising performance. Structured pruning, as employed in the PIT algorithm, eliminates unimportant output channels by training binary masks, resulting in models that are up to 9× smaller than their unoptimized counterparts [7, RissoNAS]. Furthermore, slimmable neural networks, which dynamically adjust their width based on operational context, minimize memory overhead while maintaining performance, making them suitable for resource-limited platforms [14, Slimmable-Early-Exit-Nano-Drones-Marra].

Energy efficiency is paramount for nano-drones, which operate on limited battery power. For example, the GAP8 System-on-Chip (SoC) used in the Crazyflie 2.1 nano-drone achieves real-time processing at 6 fps with an average power consumption of just 64 mW, scaling up to 18 fps at 284 mW [5, Palossi-PULP-DroNet]. 10. Limitations and Challenges

10.1 Computational Intensity of Optimization

The optimization of deep neural networks (DNNs) for nano-drones is a computationally intensive process, often requiring significant resources to achieve the desired balance between accuracy, latency, and memory usage. Techniques such as Neural Architecture Search (NAS) and structured pruning, while effective, demand extensive computational power during the training and optimization phases. For instance, the Supernet and PIT algorithms used in [7, RissoNAS] require iterative training and fine-tuning to identify the optimal network architecture, which can be resource-prohibitive for platforms with limited computational capabilities. Supernet selects between different layer types (e.g., original DW+PW block, single PW layer, standard 2DConv, or no-operation) based on trainable parameters θ, while PIT eliminates unimportant output channels by training binary masks θ [7, RissoNAS]. Additionally, the fused kernel introduced in [7, RissoNAS] reduces inference latency by up to 3.22× at iso-error by minimizing intermediate memory transfers, but it is less effective for models with many standard convolutional layers, highlighting the trade-offs involved in optimization [7, RissoNAS]. Similarly, the optimization pipeline proposed in [13, Risso-Daghero Optimized Deployment] leverages NAS and fused layer execution to reduce latency and memory usage, but the process itself is computationally demanding and may not generalize well to other hardware platforms [13, Risso-Daghero Optimized Deployment].

10.2 Robustness and Generalization Boundaries

The robustness and generalization of DNNs deployed on nano-drones are critical challenges, particularly when these models are trained in controlled environments but deployed in dynamic, real-world scenarios. The domain shift problem, where model performance degrades in environments different from the training domain, is a significant limitation. For example, the on-device fine-tuning approach proposed in [15, Cereda-OnDeviceLearning] addresses this issue by leveraging self-supervised learning based on ego-motion consistency, but it is constrained by the limited onboard sensory and computational resources of nano-drones. This method reduces horizontal position error by up to 26% compared to a non-fine-tuned baseline, consuming only 19 mW and completing in 510 ms [15, Cereda-OnDeviceLearning]. Similarly, the optimized Vision Transformers (ViTs) in [16, Jitaru - Tiny Vision Transformers] demonstrate superior generalization compared to MobileNet, achieving a 12% lower Mean Absolute Error (MAE) across all predicted axes. However, aggressive pruning can lead to a 12% decrease in performance if critical components are removed, underscoring the need for robust models that can adapt to diverse and unpredictable environments without compromising performance [16, Jitaru - Tiny Vision Transformers].

10.3 Battery Life Constraints

Battery life is a fundamental constraint for nano-drones, as their small form factor limits the size and capacity of the onboard battery. The power envelope of nano-drones is typically in the range of a few Watts, with only a fraction available for computation [4, IEEE - Nano-UAVs]. This limitation necessitates the development of energy-efficient DNNs and optimization techniques to minimize power consumption. For instance, the visual navigation engine in [5, Palossi-PULP-DroNet] achieves real-time processing at 6 fps with an average power consumption of 64 mW, scaling up to 18 fps at 284 mW, but further optimization is needed to reduce power consumption and improve reaction time for dynamic obstacles [5, Palossi-PULP-DroNet]. Similarly, the slimmable model proposed in [14, Slimmable-Early-Exit-Nano-Drones-Marra] dynamically adjusts its computational complexity based on operational context, optimizing resource usage under stringent battery constraints. This model allows for dynamic selection of four width configurations (0.25×, 0.5×, 0.75×, 1.0×) for layers after the 6th feature extractor block, minimizing memory overhead while maintaining performance [14, Slimmable-Early-Exit-Nano-Drones-Marra]. However, the effectiveness of such models in real-world applications depends on their ability to balance computational demands with energy efficiency.

10.4 Simulator Fidelity Limitations

The fidelity of simulators used for training and testing DNNs on nano-drones is another significant challenge. Simulators are essential for developing and validating models in controlled environments, but discrepancies between simulated and real-world conditions can limit the effectiveness of these models. For example, the Deep Reinforcement Learning (DRL) approach in [6, IEEE - Optimization of Deep Neural Networks for Nano-Drones] relies on simulator acceleration and randomization to train agile flight policies, but the empirical robustness of DRL algorithms is bounded by simulation fidelity and training time. This approach improves performance by up to 95% compared to state-of-the-art methods, achieving real-time inference with a latency of 2.5 ms [6, IEEE - Optimization of Deep Neural Networks for Nano-Drones]. Similarly, the benchmark for system identification in [19, Busetto-NanoDroneBenchmark] highlights the limitations of standard quadratic motor models in capturing low-frequency actuation dynamics, which residual feedforward corrections alone cannot fully compensate. The benchmark demonstrates that current methods achieve high-fidelity prediction of position and linear velocity, but accurate modeling of rotational dynamics remains a significant open challenge [19, Busetto-NanoDroneBenchmark]. These limitations emphasize the need for improved simulation techniques to address the challenges in modeling and control of nano-drones.

11. Research Opportunities

11.1 Advanced Pruning Techniques

Pruning remains a critical area of research for optimizing deep neural networks (DNNs) on nano-drones, given their stringent computational, memory, and power constraints. While structured pruning methods like the PIT algorithm have shown promise in reducing model size and latency [7, RissoNAS], there is significant potential for further advancements. Additionally, integrating pruning with other optimization strategies, such as quantization and kernel fusion, could lead to synergistic improvements in efficiency. Research could also focus on adaptive pruning methods that dynamically adjust the pruning rate based on the operational context, such as battery life or task complexity [14, Slimmable-Early-Exit-Nano-Drones-Marra]. Recent advancements in Neural Architecture Search (NAS) algorithms, such as Supernet and PIT, have demonstrated significant improvements in model compression and latency reduction, achieving up to 9× size compression and 3.22× latency reduction in visual pose estimation tasks [7, RissoNAS]. However, the optimization process is computationally intensive, and the fused kernel’s benefits are less pronounced for models with many standard convolutional layers, highlighting the need for further research in this area.

11.2 Enhanced Federated Learning

Federated learning (FL) offers a promising approach to training DNNs on nano-drones by enabling collaborative learning without centralized data aggregation. However, current FL frameworks face challenges such as communication overhead, data heterogeneity, and limited computational resources [10, Tiny-DroNeRF]. Future research could focus on developing lightweight FL algorithms that minimize communication costs while maintaining model accuracy. Additionally, integrating FL with edge computing architectures could enable real-time model updates and improve scalability [17, DTOE-AOF]. Another promising direction is the development of privacy-preserving FL methods, such as differential privacy or secure multi-party computation, to address data security concerns in sensitive applications like surveillance and inspection [18, DTOE-AOF]. The Dynamic Task Offloading Edge-Aware Optimization Framework (DTOE-AOF) has demonstrated significant improvements in mission efficiency, response time, and resource utilization by leveraging federated learning and edge computing, achieving 99.4% mission efficiency and 98.9% reduction in response time [17, DTOE-AOF]. However, challenges such as ensuring data security during transmission and managing UAV mobility remain areas for further exploration.

11.3 Cross-Platform Generalization

The ability to generalize DNN models across different nano-drone platforms and hardware architectures is a critical research opportunity. Current optimization techniques are often tailored to specific hardware, such as the GAP8 SoC, limiting their applicability to other platforms [13, Risso-Daghero Optimized Deployment]. Techniques like slimmable networks, which dynamically adjust model width based on available resources, could be extended to support multiple hardware configurations [14, Slimmable-Early-Exit-Nano-Drones-Marra]. The Crazyflie 2.1 Brushless nano-quadrotor benchmark provides a comprehensive dataset and evaluation protocol for system identification, facilitating transparent comparison of algorithms and supporting research on agile, miniaturized aerial robotics [19, Busetto-NanoDroneBenchmark]. However, the need for more expressive physics-based rotor models and temporal learning architectures remains an open challenge.

11.4 Hybrid Physics-Learning Models

Hybrid models that combine physics-based approaches with deep learning offer a promising avenue for improving the accuracy and efficiency of nano-drone systems. While purely data-driven models have shown success in tasks like system identification and control, they exhibit higher errors in recursive prediction due to compounding errors [20, Busetto-NanoDroneSysID]. Hybrid models, which integrate physical priors with neural networks, could address these limitations by leveraging domain knowledge to guide learning. For example, incorporating aerodynamic equations or motor dynamics into DNN architectures could improve the prediction of flight trajectories and control responses [19, Busetto-NanoDroneBenchmark]. Research could also explore the use of hybrid models for tasks like 3D scene reconstruction, where collaborative training and model compression techniques have shown potential [10, Tiny-DroNeRF]. Additionally, developing methods to manually refine physical priors and residual components could improve the design of hybrid models, making them more effective for real-world applications. The Crazyflie 2.1 Brushless nano-quadrotor benchmark demonstrates that hybrid models combining physics and neural residuals deliver the best performance in system identification tasks, highlighting the potential of hybrid approaches in improving prediction accuracy and robustness [19, Busetto-NanoDroneBenchmark]. However, the limitations of the standard quadratic motor model and the need for more expressive physics-based rotor models suggest areas for further research.

12. Analysis of Key Works

12.1 RissoNAS: Visual Pose Estimation Optimization

RissoNAS, proposed by Matteo Risso et al., addresses the challenge of deploying deep neural networks (DNNs) for visual pose estimation on nano-drones, which are constrained by limited computational, memory, and power resources. The authors introduce an optimization pipeline that leverages Neural Architecture Search (NAS) algorithms—Supernet and PIT—to automatically discover optimal DNN architectures. The pipeline also includes a fused kernel for efficient execution of DepthWise (DW) and PointWise (PW) convolution layers, reducing intermediate memory transfers and improving latency. The optimized models achieve significant improvements, with the smallest model achieving a 9× size compression at iso-MAE compared to the most accurate state-of-the-art (SoTA) model, while the largest model improves MAE by up to 13.78%. The fused kernel reduces inference latency by up to 3.22× at iso-error, making it feasible for real-time applications on nano-drones [7, RissoNAS] [13, Risso-Daghero Optimized Deployment].

12.2 BLANKSKIP: Adaptive Object Detection

BLANKSKIP is an adaptive deep neural network designed for on-device object detection on nano-drones. It addresses the severe computational, memory, and power constraints of nano-drone platforms, which typically have ≈10 MiB memory and ≈1 W power budgets, by leveraging an early-exit mechanism. This mechanism identifies frames that do not contain objects of interest, bypassing the full detection pipeline for such frames. This approach is particularly beneficial in scenarios where a significant fraction of frames are empty, such as exploration or inspection tasks. By reducing the average inference latency, BLANKSKIP enables efficient object detection on resource-constrained platforms, making it suitable for real-time applications [8, BLANKSKIP].

12.3 PULP-DroNet: Visual Navigation Engine

PULP-DroNet, developed by Palossi et al., is a visual navigation engine for autonomous nano-drones. It leverages deep neural networks to enable closed-loop end-to-end visual navigation on resource-constrained platforms. The system is deployed on the GAP8 parallel ultra-low-power computing platform and the CrazyFlie 2.0 nano-quadrotor. The authors reduce the numerical representation of weights and activations from 32-bit floating point to 16-bit fixed point and extend the training dataset to include images from the onboard ultra-low-power camera. The system achieves real-time processing at 6 fps with an average power consumption of 64 mW, scaling up to 18 fps at 284 mW. The navigation engine demonstrates comparable control accuracy to the baseline DroNet implementation on standard-sized drones, making it suitable for autonomous navigation tasks on nano-drones [5, Palossi-PULP-DroNet].

12.4 Tiny Vision Transformers for Pose Estimation

The thesis by Jitaru explores the optimization and application of Vision Transformers (ViTs) for human pose estimation on nano-drones. The study focuses on optimizing ViTs for deployment on nano-drones equipped with a GAP8 AI deck. The methodology includes dataset preparation, model optimization, and structured pruning to reduce the model size while preserving performance. The optimized ViT achieves a 12% lower Mean Absolute Error (MAE) compared to MobileNet across all predicted axes (x, y, z, φ). Structured pruning results in a 30% reduction in the number of parameters, with only a 12% decrease in performance. The ViT model demonstrates superior generalization ability, making it suitable for real-time applications on resource-constrained nano-drones [16, Jitaru - Tiny Vision Transformers].

12.5 Slimmable Early-Exit Networks

Carlo Marra’s thesis explores the deployment of slimmable neural networks for object detection on nano-drones. The proposed approach modifies the MobileNetV2 SSDLite model into a slimmable model, allowing for dynamic selection of four width configurations (0.25×, 0.5×, 0.75×, 1.0×) for layers after the 6th feature extractor block. The slimmable model dynamically adjusts its computational complexity and resource usage based on the operational context, optimizing performance under the stringent constraints of nano-drones. The shared weight mechanism ensures that the memory footprint remains minimal, making it feasible for deployment on resource-limited platforms [14, Slimmable-Early-Exit-Nano-Drones-Marra].

12.6 Adaptive Deep Learning for Pose Estimation

The paper “Adaptive Deep Learning for Efficient Visual Pose Estimation aboard Ultra-low-power Nano-drones” proposes an adaptive deep learning mechanism for efficient execution of vision-based human pose estimation tasks on nano-drones. The methodology involves leveraging two State-of-the-Art (SoA) convolutional neural networks (CNNs) with different performance-computation trade-offs and combining them with three novel adaptation strategies based on temporal consistency and auxiliary tasks. The best-performing system achieves a 28% reduction in latency while maintaining the same mean absolute error (MAE) compared to executing only the larger and more accurate SoA model. The approach significantly reduces latency and energy consumption while maintaining or improving accuracy, making it suitable for real-time applications on resource-constrained nano-drones [9, AdaptiveDL-NanoDrones].

12.7 NanoDroneBenchmark: System Identification

The NanoDroneBenchmark, introduced by Busetto et al., provides a benchmark for system identification on nano-drones, specifically focusing on the Crazyflie 2.1 Brushless nano-quadrotor. The benchmark is based on 75,000 real-world samples collected from aggressive flight trajectories, including synchronized 4-dimensional motor inputs and 13-dimensional output measurements. The dataset is designed to challenge system identification methods due to the platform’s multi-input, multi-output nature, open-loop instability, and nonlinear dynamics during agile maneuvers. The benchmark includes a suite of multi-horizon prediction metrics for evaluating both one-step and multi-step error propagation, facilitating transparent comparison of algorithms and supporting research on agile, miniaturized aerial robotics [19, Busetto-NanoDroneBenchmark] [20, Busetto-NanoDroneSysID].

12.8 DTOE-AOF: Dynamic Task Offloading Framework

The Dynamic Task Offloading Edge-Aware Optimization Framework (DTOE-AOF) optimizes UAV operations by integrating AI-driven decision-making with edge computing. The framework dynamically allocates computational tasks between UAVs and edge nodes based on proximity, available resources, and task urgency, reducing latency, improving mission efficiency, and conserving onboard resources. DTOE-AOF leverages federated learning for data privacy, reinforcement learning for real-time adaptability, and Graph Neural Networks (GNNs) for optimizing resource allocation and scheduling. Simulation studies demonstrate that DTOE-AOF outperforms traditional centralized methods in mission efficiency, response time, and resource utilization, making it a promising solution for optimizing UAV operations in dynamic and resource-constrained environments [17, DTOE-AOF] [18, DTOE-AOF].

12.9 Tiny-DroNeRF: 3D Scene Reconstruction

Tiny-DroNeRF is a lightweight Neural Radiance Fields (NeRF) model optimized for deployment on nano-drones equipped with ultra-low-power microcontroller units (MCUs). The model is optimized through hyperparameter tuning to balance reconstruction quality, memory usage, and computational efficiency. Tiny-DroNeRF achieves a 96% reduction in memory footprint compared to Instant-NGP, reducing memory usage from 527 MB to 21.4 MB, with only a 5.7 dB drop in PSNR. 12.10 On-device Self-supervised Learning

The paper “Training on the Fly: On-device Self-supervised Learning aboard Nano-drones within 20 mW” by Elia Cereda et al. proposes a novel on-device fine-tuning approach that leverages ultra-low power resources available on nano-drones to mitigate the domain shift problem. The methodology involves fine-tuning a pre-trained convolutional neural network (CNN) on-device using a self-supervised learning approach based on ego-motion consistency, which eliminates the need for ground-truth labels. The proposed method significantly reduces horizontal position error by up to 26% compared to a non-fine-tuned state-of-the-art baseline. The on-device training procedure requires only 1 MB of memory and consumes as low as 19 mW, completing in just 510 ms. The approach enables the nano-drone to successfully complete missions in challenging, never-seen-before environments where the baseline fails, making it highly practical for real-world applications [15, Cereda-OnDeviceLearning].
